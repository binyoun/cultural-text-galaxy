Writing Guide

Research Foundation

The radial formation is not decorative. It is drawn from a specific precedent that fits the workshop's own concept: naming as a way of claiming a place in the sky.

Primary reference

Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido (천상열차분야지도)

A 14th-century Korean stone star chart. It places the celestial pole at the center of the stone and arranges constellations in rings radiating outward from it. It was carved under a Joseon king partly as a claim of sovereignty over the sky itself. A center point with names radiating outward carries the same structure, and the same idea: naming what is yours to name.

Secondary reference

Fan and pedigree charts

Circular genealogy diagrams that radiate outward from a person through their family line. Closer to the poem's own list of names, family, neighbors, and distant relatives, than a pure star chart is.

Supporting principle

The non-hierarchical circle

Writing in a ring, with no first or last position, is a documented pattern across many communal and ritual practices, used specifically so no name outranks another. It supports the workshop's own claim that different scripts and languages coexist without being flattened into a single order.

How to add your name to the sky

  1. Find an open ray on the circle.
  2. Write your name along it. Let the top of your letters point outward, away from the center.
  3. Write in your own language, your own script. Nothing here needs translating.
  4. Leave the center clear. It stays open, shared by everyone.
  5. We will photograph your name and send it into the galaxy on screen.
Inspired by Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido, a centuries-old Korean star chart, and Yoon Dong-ju's 1941 poem "Counting the Stars at Night."

Underlay Template

A shared master sheet, not a per-person handout. Place a blank sheet over this guide (or scale it up to your final communal sheet size) so each participant writes along an open ray in turn. Twenty rays, one per expected participant. The center stays empty.
