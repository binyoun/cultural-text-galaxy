Writing Guide

How a name becomes a photograph becomes two things

Each participant writes their own name on their own paper, following this guide, then photographs it once. That one photograph goes two places: into TouchDesigner for their individual piece, and into the shared pool for the communal work shown at the workshop and kept afterward as documentation. The writing formation has to hold up at both scales, a single name on its own, and dozens of names together, so it is built on a form that already does exactly that.

Research Foundation: Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido (천상열차분야지도)

A chart rebuilt after loss

Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido is a Korean star chart carved in stone around 1395, in the first years of the Joseon dynasty. It was not drawn from scratch. It reproduces an older chart said to date to Goguryeo, whose original stone was lost, reportedly during the fall of the kingdom. What survived was a paper rubbing. Joseon court astronomers used that rubbing to reconstruct the chart, correcting star positions for the centuries that had passed since the original observations, then re-carved it in stone. A record of the sky disappeared with a kingdom and was carried forward anyway, on paper, until someone had the authority and the occasion to put it back into permanent form. That is close to what this workshop is already asking of its participants.

Field allocation: sky as claimed territory

The chart's own name contains its method. Bunya (분야, 分野) means "field allocation," a system used across East Asian astronomy that divides the sky into sectors and assigns each one to a region of earth below it. A patch of stars is not just a patch of stars, it corresponds to a place, and by extension to the people and the authority tied to that place. Mapping the sky was a way of mapping, and claiming, the ground. This is the same move ISEA's Charting Constellations pillar asks for, reshaping geography and identity through a digital, multidimensional lens, except Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido did it in stone six hundred years earlier. Applied to this workshop: each participant's name is their own field allocation, a claimed patch of sky that is theirs by the act of writing it.

A claim of legitimacy through naming the sky

Re-carving the chart was also a political act. A new dynasty demonstrating command over astronomical knowledge, the movements of heaven, was a recognized way of asserting the legitimacy of its rule. Naming and ordering the stars was not neutral observation, it was a declaration of authority. Set against sōshi-kaimei, the colonial policy that stripped Koreans of their own names, the contrast is direct: one system used naming to assert power over a people, the other used naming the sky to assert a people's own authority. Yoon Dong-ju's poem sits on the second side of that line. So does this workshop.

Structure: one pole, many named stars

The chart is organized around a fixed point near the center, corresponding to the celestial pole, with the twenty-eight lunar mansions and hundreds of individually catalogued constellations arranged in bands radiating outward from it. Every star that mattered was named individually and placed in relation to that center, not scattered at random and not lined up in a row. This is the direct source for the workshop's own radial writing form: a fixed, empty center, and each name given its own position, its own ray, radiating outward from that shared point.

From one chart to many

A single participant's name, written with each character or syllable placed along its own ray around a personal center, is a small Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido of one person, their own field allocation, their own claimed sky. That photograph is what TouchDesigner processes individually. When every participant's chart is gathered for the communal work, the structure repeats at a larger scale, many personal skies contributing to one shared one, which is the same relationship the original chart has to the twelve field allocations, one system, many claimed territories, held together without erasing any of them.

How to write your name

  1. Mark a small center point on your paper. Leave it empty. It is your fixed point, your pole star.
  2. Write your name one character or syllable at a time, each one on its own ray, spreading outward from that center like a clock face.
  3. Let the top of each character point outward, away from the center.
  4. Write in your own language, your own script. Nothing here needs translating.
  5. When you are done, photograph the whole page. This one photograph becomes both your individual piece and part of the shared galaxy.
Inspired by Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido, a Korean star chart carved in 1395, and Yoon Dong-ju's 1941 poem "Counting the Stars at Night."
