Conceptual Foundation

Naming as Cultural Resistance

This workshop takes its inspiration from Yoon Dong-ju's 1941 poem Counting the Stars at Night (별 헤는 밤), written during the Japanese colonial occupation of Korea, when Korean identity was systematically suppressed. Under the sōshi-kaimei (創氏改名) policy, Koreans were forced to adopt Japanese names, an erasure of lineage, language, and belonging. In this context, Dong-ju's poem becomes an act of resistance: he counts stars by naming them after schoolchildren, neighbors, and distant family members. Each name is a declaration of existence.

Names are like stars. They can be connected with others in cultural constellations.

When participants write their family names, neighbors' names, or the names of places in their own languages, they create material for transformation. The handwritten text becomes image. The image becomes particles. The particles become a navigational constellation. This is both a technical process, understanding an image-to-particle workflow, and a conceptual one: how cultural text can flow, drift, and reconstellate as visual poetry.

Alignment with ISEA 2026: ELYAH

Charting Constellations

"Explores concepts of place and identity in the digital age and how we reshape our understanding of geography, identity, and community in a multidimensional world."

This workshop responds by transforming handwritten names, embodied markers of place and belonging, into three-dimensional particle systems. Participants learn how computational behaviors such as drift, attraction, and turbulence can represent the experience of cultural displacement and reconnection, discovering how to chart constellations of identity through hands-on, real-time generative practice.

Celestial Dialogues

"Examines how artistic and technological practices can foster intercultural dialogue and shared cross-cultural experiences."

In Dubai, a city where Arabic, Hindi, Urdu, Tagalog, Malayalam, Farsi, Korean, and dozens of other scripts coexist, the workshop produces a poly-scriptural cosmos. Each writing system carries its own visual density and rhythm. The workshop does not flatten these differences but lets them coexist in the same volumetric space. The final collective visualization, where every participant's name orbits together, is intercultural dialogue made visible in shared computational space.
